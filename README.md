# Early-Onset Colorectal Cancer: A Critical Appraisal

**Evaluating the strength of epidemiological evidence from an Ontario case–control study.**

[Read the full appraisal](Critical_Appraisal.pdf) · [View the original study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10552-021-01456-8) · [Author's GitHub](https://github.com/sy0203)

## Overview

This project critically appraises a population-based case–control study by **Chang et al. (2021)** examining medical, lifestyle and dietary factors associated with early-onset colorectal cancer in Ontario, Canada.

The report assesses how study design, participant recruitment, exposure measurement and statistical adjustment affect confidence in the findings. Its central focus is the distinction between a reported association and convincing evidence of a causal relationship.

**My contribution:** I evaluated the published study's methods, interpreted its adjusted estimates and confidence intervals, and synthesized its strengths and limitations in a structured written appraisal. All participant data and statistical estimates discussed here come from the original researchers; this repository contains the appraisal rather than an independent reanalysis.

## Study at a glance

| Element | Original study |
| --- | --- |
| Design | Population-based case–control study |
| Setting | Ontario, Canada; 2018–2019 |
| Population | Adults aged 20–49 |
| Sample | **428 participants:** 175 colorectal cancer cases and 253 controls |
| Case identification | Ontario Cancer Registry, with pathology-confirmed diagnoses |
| Control recruitment | Random-digit dialing, with matching by sex and five-year age group |
| Exposure measurement | Self-administered online questionnaire covering medical history, lifestyle and diet |
| Main analysis | Logistic regression estimating adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals |

## What the appraisal evaluates

| Area | Questions examined in the report |
| --- | --- |
| Study design and sampling | Does the case–control design suit the research question? Do the controls adequately represent the population that produced the cases? |
| Measurement and timing | How do registry-confirmed diagnoses compare with retrospectively self-reported exposures? Could recall error or early symptoms influence the findings? |
| Confounding and missing data | Which covariates were adjusted for, how were additional confounders assessed, and how were missing responses handled? |
| Statistical interpretation | What do adjusted odds ratios, confidence intervals and subgroup analyses reveal about the size and uncertainty of associations? |
| Validity and applicability | How might selection bias, residual confounding and participant characteristics limit causal interpretation and generalizability? |

## Selected findings from the original study

The following are **published results reviewed in the appraisal**, not estimates generated for this project.

| Exposure comparison | Adjusted odds ratio | 95% confidence interval |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Family history of colorectal cancer in a first- or second-degree relative, versus none | 2.37 | 1.47–3.84 |
| Sedentary time of at least 10 hours/day, versus less than 5 hours/day | 1.93 | 1.02–3.65 |
| Sugary drink consumption of at least 7 drinks/week, versus less than 1 drink/week | 2.99 | 1.57–5.68 |

**How to interpret these numbers:** An odds ratio above 1 indicates higher odds of the outcome in the exposure group relative to the comparison group, after adjustment for the model's covariates. For example, 2.99 represents approximately three times the odds. The confidence intervals show uncertainty around these estimates; they do not account for every possible source of bias.

These associations do not establish causation or directly quantify an individual's absolute risk.

## Main appraisal findings

### Strengths supporting the evidence

- **Reliable case identification:** Registry and pathology information provide a clearly defined, confirmed outcome.
- **Broad exposure coverage:** The questionnaire considers medical history, lifestyle and diet, allowing several plausible associations to be examined.
- **Explicit statistical reporting:** Adjusted estimates and confidence intervals make effect sizes and precision available for evaluation.

### Limitations affecting interpretation

- **Selective participation:** Of 782 eligible cases, 175 completed the questionnaire; 253 of approximately 1,800 eligible controls completed it. Participation could be related to the exposures under investigation.
- **Recall and measurement error:** Retrospective reports of diet, sedentary time and medication use may inaccurately represent earlier exposures.
- **Uncertain temporal relationships:** Asking about earlier exposures helps address timing, but prediagnostic symptoms could still affect some reported behaviours.
- **Residual confounding and multiple comparisons:** Adjustment cannot guarantee that all relevant differences are controlled, and examining many associations increases the possibility of chance findings.

### Overall assessment

The study offers useful evidence of associations worth investigating further. Confidence in its conclusions depends on weighing strong outcome ascertainment against limitations in participation and exposure measurement. Replication and stronger temporal evidence would help determine whether the observed relationships reflect causal effects.
